H3 PAC: Dissolved, But Not Before Funding the Attack Ads

Two brothers' lobbying firm fed $134,400 into a PAC that funded 56+ Republican candidates statewide — then a related, nearly-identically-named entity ran attack ads against three Democratic candidates while reporting zero dollars raised or spent.

1. What Is This PAC?

H3 PAC was created July 21, 2023 by brothers Greg and Jeff Hartley as the successor to an earlier PAC, Utahns for Safer Communities — same address, 52 E 300 N, Salt Lake City. Jeff Hartley owns Sage Government Solutions LLC, a lobbying firm whose registered clients include Liberty Village Utah, OpenAI, ClearRoad Inc., Okta, VMware, and others — while it simultaneously represents the Utah Inland Port Authority and private developers seeking UIPA project-area designations.

2. The Attack-Ad Network

Attack ads and mailers paid for by "Utahns for Safer Communities" — H3 PAC's own predecessor entity, co-founded by the same Hartley brothers — appeared in three separate 2026 races: against Scott Stephenson (HD-44, already documented in this project's Checks & Balances PAC file), and against Angela Choberka and Rosemary Lesser (both documented in this project's UINPAC file). In every case, the PAC reported $0 in contributions and $0 in expenditures for the relevant 2026 periods — a documented compliance discrepancy, not an assumption.

A separate but related dark-money vehicle: Preserving Utah Values PAC

Not the same entity as H3 PAC or Utahns for Safer Communities, but part of the same attack-ad pattern against the same three candidates. Preserving Utah Values PAC is Wisconsin-registered (Hudson, WI; officers Thomas and Timothy Datwyler), created October 2024, and received its sole documented contribution — $120,000 — from Mike Schultz personally (11/30/2024). It filed a dissolution Final Report in June 2025, yet a report filed the same day shows $2,914.57 in expenditures — a contradiction in its own paperwork. It ran zero-disclosure attack ads against Stephenson, Choberka, and Lesser (during her 2024 race, which she lost by roughly 300 votes). The Salt Lake Tribune independently named Schultz as the funder, using his own photo.

Two nearly-identical PAC names exist simultaneously and should not be confused: "Protect Utah Values PAC" (Utah-registered, Springville, aka "Remove Cox," funded by Robert K. Clayson) is a different, unrelated entity from "Preserving Utah Values PAC" (Wisconsin).

H3 PAC's July 2026 dissolution followed a clean-exit pattern already seen elsewhere in this project: move the remaining balance out before filing to dissolve. With a starting balance of $12,235 and zero new contributions that cycle, it sent $2,500 each to UHREC and the Utah Republican Senate Committee, $5,000 to Adams Leadership PAC, and $2,235 back to its own predecessor, Utahns for Safer Communities — the same entity that went on to run 2026 attack ads reporting $0.

4. Cross-Network Convergence

H3 PAC doesn't operate in isolation — several of its recipients also appear, independently, in other PAC networks already documented in this project.

Scott Sandall — a four-network convergence point

H3 PAC recipient, plus $7,500 from Lincoln Hill Partners (2025), $9,000 from Sen. Chris Wilson's PAC (2026), and UFAIR board chair per separate research in this project. Four separate funding/institutional threads converge on one legislator.

Jefferson Moss — both PAC and personal-payment recipient

H3 PAC recipient, and separately received direct personal payments (not campaign contributions) from Lincoln Hill Partners: $1,000 (2023), $2,000 (2024) — connecting to a direct-payment pattern already flagged in this project's separate Jefferson Moss state-capture research.

Casey Snider — recipient and NUPAC insider, simultaneously

Named officer of NUPAC (per this project's Koford/NUPAC research) while also an H3 PAC recipient — an insider and a recipient at the same time, not a passive, arms-length funding relationship.

5. Worth Asking

  • A lobbying firm representing UIPA and private developers seeking UIPA designations simultaneously funded a PAC that gave to 56+ legislators who vote on UIPA-adjacent policy. Is that an ordinary part of how lobbying and political giving work together, or does the dual representation change how it should be read?
  • The zero-disclosure attack ads ran under a predecessor entity's name, not H3 PAC's own — after H3 PAC itself had already dissolved and moved its money elsewhere. Does dissolving the funding vehicle before the disclosure-avoiding activity happens make accountability harder to trace, even if each individual step is separately legal?

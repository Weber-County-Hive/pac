Chris McConnehey: Swept In By Accident, Funded By Design

He shows up in this project's Checks & Balances PAC file purely because he shares a funder with two other candidates — he has no opponent tie to the judicial-power fight at all. His own campaign tells a completely different, and more interesting, story: a signature-gathering machine, a former Speaker's personal money, and a network that keeps reappearing across this whole project.

New here? Chris McConnehey is the Republican candidate for Utah House District 38 (West Jordan), an open seat left by retiring incumbent Cheryl Acton. He first appeared in this project as a side note in the Checks & Balances PAC case file — that PAC gave him $4,000, the same PAC funding primary challengers against two legislators behind a 2026 judicial-power overhaul. But McConnehey has no opponent relationship to either of those legislators and no connection to that fight. He's here purely because he shares a funder. His own campaign's real story — who actually funded him, and how — is documented for the first time on this page.

1. What Is This Race?

McConnehey lost the April 2026 Republican convention badly to Gloria Vindas, 29% to 71% of delegates — a wide enough margin that under Utah's old convention-only nomination system, his campaign would have ended there. Instead, under the signature-gathering path created by SB54 (2014), he advanced to the June primary and won it convincingly, 62.4% to 37.6%. He now faces unaffiliated candidate Sergio Sotelo in November. Utah News Dispatch grouped HD-38 with HD-44 (the Teuscher/Stephenson race documented in this project's Checks & Balances PAC file) as one of several "contentious" 2026 primaries featuring AI-generated attack ads — a genuinely high-intensity race, not a sleepy open seat.

This same underperform-at-convention, win-the-primary shape shows up across this entire project. Kelly Smith, Scott Stephenson, and Jake Hunsaker all show the identical pattern in their own files. 2. His Real Funder: CMV PAC

Checks & Balances PAC gave McConnehey $4,000 — the connection that pulled him into this project in the first place. But that's not close to his largest source of money. CMV PAC (Count My Vote) gave him $12,750 across two in-kind payments — more than triple what Checks & Balances PAC gave him, and 24.9% of everything he raised.

CMV PAC's own case file in this project documents it as a large, systematic distributor — a standardized $5,625 "signature gathering" package paid to at least a dozen Republican candidates statewide in a single January 2026 batch, several of whom already have their own case files in this project (Jim Dunnigan, Raymond Ward, Erik Craythorne, Sam Barlow, Jake Hunsaker). McConnehey's first CMV PAC payment is the exact standard rate; the second, larger payment four months later brings his total well above what most other recipients got.

3. The Signature-Gathering Network

McConnehey's campaign paid "Gathering, Inc" $2,700 for signature gathering (4/23/26) — the same service CMV PAC's own in-kind payment covers. Confirmed via the company's own Utah Division of Corporations filing: its officers are Casey Hill (co-founder of Lincoln Hill Partners and a registered Election Hive lobbyist, already extensively documented elsewhere in this project) and Spencer Stokes (President of Stokes Strategies, a lobbying firm) — both listed at the same Salt Lake City address. McConnehey's campaign is a direct, paying customer of a network already documented in this project's Casey Hill/Derek Brown case file, not just a beneficiary of a PAC that happens to fund similar candidates.

Spencer Stokes' co-ownership connects to a second McConnehey funder through a different route. Stokes previously co-founded "Utah Term Limits" in 2020 with Dan Hemmert — a PAC the Salt Lake Tribune called "fake," created one day after a disclosure deadline specifically to hide its donors before a primary. Hemmert is separately confirmed on this page as Treasurer of All In For Utah PAC, which itself gave McConnehey $5,643.55 in-kind (see Section 4). Two people with a documented history of a disclosure-evading PAC together now connect to two different McConnehey funding sources.

A statewide search of 2026 Gathering Inc expenditures turned up a much bigger operation than one HD-38 candidate. Its largest identified 2026 client, at $76,682 across six payments, is filed under "Adams, J." Senate President Stuart Adams' full legal name is Jabez Stuart Adams, formally styled "J. Stuart Adams" in every official context — an exact match to this filing format. Worth noting the real-world outcome: Adams lost his own June 2026 Republican primary to Stephanie Hollist, 43%-35%, despite this spending — a data point for the same "money buys ballot access, not necessarily victory" pattern already visible elsewhere on this page and across this project. Other 2026 Gathering Inc clients include payments filed under "Sloan, Chris," "Wurth, Dakota," "Stephenson, Jon," and an entity called "Utahns for Representative Government" — a name close enough to "Utahns for Responsive Government" (an unresearched donor elsewhere in this project) to be worth checking, though not yet confirmed as the same group.

His own campaign site's endorsement list

McConnehey's endorsers, from his own campaign site, back up the financial picture above with a public roster of political relationships: Chad Lamb, Zach Jacob, Dave Newton, Lorin Palmer, Dr. David Pack, Jim Bird, and Rob Bennett (current and former West Jordan/Herriman local officials), plus three names that connect directly to money already documented on this page or elsewhere in this project — Wayne Harper (Utah State Senator), Jim Dunnigan (Utah State Representative, also a $700 donor — see Transaction Explorer), and Annette Harris (West Jordan City Council, also a $100 donor). Also endorsing: Aimee Winder Newton (Salt Lake County Council Chair), Dirk Burton (West Jordan Mayor), Niki George (Jordan School Board President).

This resolves, at least partially, an open question from this project's UINPAC file. UINPAC (a Democratic-aligned PAC) separately lists an "Annette Harris" as a political-support recipient, and it was an open question whether she's the same person as McConnehey's $100 donor of the same name. His own site identifies his Annette Harris as a sitting West Jordan City Council member personally endorsing his Republican campaign — a poor fit for a Democratic PAC's recipient, though city council seats are often officially nonpartisan, so this isn't a certain rule-out without an address comparison.

6. Worth Asking

  • McConnehey lost his own party's convention by a wide margin, then won comfortably once the money-fueled signature-gathering path put him on the primary ballot. Is that the system working as designed — giving primary voters more say than a smaller pool of convention delegates — or is it a way for money to manufacture a win the party's most engaged members didn't want?
  • The same handful of names — Scott Anderson, Dan Hemmert, Casey Hill's network, Matthew Sanderson's entities — keep reappearing across completely separate races and PACs in this project. Is that evidence of a genuinely small number of people who fund and staff most competitive Utah Republican campaigns, regardless of district? If so, what does "constituent service" mean to a legislator whose viability depended on a repeat cast of statewide funders rather than local voters?

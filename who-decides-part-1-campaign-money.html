Who Decides, Part 1: Campaign Money, and the Leaders Who Run the Caucus PACs

Utah sets no dollar limit on gifts to state candidates. The Legislature writes the money rules, and its own leaders are the listed officers of the caucus PACs that fund legislative races. This page lays out who the players are, what changed from 2010 to 2026, and how each change grades for the public.

1. Start Here

IN PLAIN WORDSLawmakers write the rules for how campaigns raise and spend money. In Utah those rules do not cap how much anyone can give a state candidate. Instead, campaigns have to report what they take in and spend. The two parties in the Legislature each run political action committees (PACs) that collect money and give it to candidates. State filings list the parties’ own legislative leaders as the officers of those PACs. State law allows this.

Tap a card to flip it.

$0DOLLAR LIMIT ON A GIFT
Utah sets no cap on what a person, PAC, business, union or party can give a state candidate (Ballotpedia, citing NCSL).
~11STATES WITH NO LIMIT
As of Feb. 2023, NCSL counted about 11 states with no limit on gifts from individuals to state candidates. Utah is one. Oregon’s first limits start in 2027.
3LIMIT BILLS THAT DIED
2014 H.B. 297 (failed 35-38), 2014 H.B. 237 (never voted on), 2015 H.B. 60 (failed 31-40).
2OFFICERS WITH THE POWER
Every PAC must name two officers with “primary decision-making authority” (Utah Code § 20A-11-601). Nothing in that section bars legislators.

2. Words Used on This Page

PAC (political action committee)

A fund that collects money and gives it to candidates or spends it on campaigns. It must file reports with the Lt. Governor’s Office.

Caucus PAC

A PAC run for one party’s members in the House or Senate. Examples: the Utah House Republican Election Committee (UHREC) and SenDemPAC.

Leadership PAC

A PAC sponsored by one lawmaker, often a leader. Examples: the House Speaker Leadership PAC and Adams Leadership PAC.

Primary officers

The two people a PAC must name as having “primary decision-making authority.” No one else may use that authority (§ 20A-11-601(5)).

Supermajority

Two-thirds of the members of a chamber: 50 of 75 in the House, 20 of 29 in the Senate. It takes two-thirds of each house to override a governor’s veto (Utah Constitution, Art. VII, Sec. 8), or to make a bill take effect the day it is signed.

Personal use

Spending campaign money on the candidate’s own costs. Banned in Utah since 2010 (§ 20A-11-104).

3. Who the Players Are

Who writes the rules

The Legislature. Contribution limits, report dates and penalties are set in Utah Code Title 20A, Chapter 11. A change needs a majority of each house and the governor’s signature, or two-thirds of each house to override a veto.

Both parties use the same setup. The Senate Democrats’ PAC is led by its own leaders, just as the Republican caucus PACs are. The Republican committees raise and spend much more.

The treasurer. The filings list Kyle Palmer, occupation “Consultant,” as treasurer of six PACs: the five in this table plus the Utah County Legislative PAC. The same PACs report paying Irish Elk LLC, which state business records list him as manager of. See PC 100.16 and PC 100.17 for the amounts.

Who enforces the rules

  • Lt. Governor’s Office, Elections (Lt. Gov. Deidre Henderson): receives state campaign reports, reviews complaints, issues guidance and can order penalties. It can refer a case to the Attorney General.
  • Attorney General’s Office: handles criminal referrals.
  • County clerks and county attorneys: handle disclosures by county officials. The State Auditor can also receive complaints about local disclosure.

7. Enforcement: Three Cases in the Record

Senate President Adams’s credit-card reporting (2024–2025)

A Dec. 2024 complaint from Alliance for a Better Utah said Adams listed credit-card companies and banks as payees instead of the final vendors. On Jan. 2, 2025 the Lt. Governor’s Office closed the complaint with no action. Its letter said state law requires the final payee to be listed, that earlier guidance had suggested less detail was enough, and that the rule would apply going forward. Adams called the claim “false and baseless” and said he followed the law and the office’s guidelines (FOX 13, Jan. 2, 2025).

Preserving Utah Values PAC and the Speaker’s $120,000 (2024–2025)

The PAC reported $0 raised and spent before the 2024 election while running ads. Its first and only donation, $120,000 from House Speaker Mike Schultz, was reported Nov. 30, 2024. Rep. Rosemary Lesser and Alliance for a Better Utah filed complaints. The Lt. Governor’s Office referred the matter to the Attorney General on Nov. 13 after the PAC didn’t respond in 7 days. The office later said the complaints were “considered resolved”; no fines were reported, and the PAC filed to dissolve in June 2025. Its treasurer apologized for a missed email and said the PAC was in compliance. The gift itself was legal, since Utah has no contribution limit (Salt Lake Tribune, Dec. 2, 2024 and Nov. 25, 2025).

Weber County Commissioner Froerer’s disclosure (2022–2023)

Froerer held a 16% interest, through a family trust, in Nordic Valley Land Associates LLC. He recused from the Aug. and Dec. 2022 votes on the land-use change, rezone and development agreement, which passed. A resident complained to the County Attorney and State Auditor. He filed an updated disclosure in Nov. 2022, and a March 2023 note said he had no stake as of Feb. 2023. The county’s civil deputy said the updated disclosures met state law, which requires disclosure, not recusal. No sanction was reported (Utah Public Radio, June 13, 2023).

The pattern in the record. In all three, complaints were received and answered, and reporting was corrected or explained. In none was a fine reported.

The other side: arguments made against limits

In the 2015 debate, Rep. Brad Wilson said limits don’t cap outside “dark money” spending, which a U.S. Supreme Court ruling put beyond reach. Reps. Fred Cox and Brad Daw said large checks let challengers compete against well-funded opponents and outside campaigns. Supporters, including Rep. King, said limits were what most Utahns wanted and cited a poll showing about 68–69% support (UtahPolicy, Jan. 27 and Mar. 3, 2015). Party and caucus committees in both parties say they exist to help their candidates win close races and build the party.

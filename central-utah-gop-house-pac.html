Central Utah GOP House PAC: Two Rosters, One Address

A small leadership PAC that has quietly moved money to dozens of Utah Republicans since 2018 — registered two different ways, with two different sets of people in charge, and its own directors among the candidates it paid.

Election-law creation: Nov. 26, 2018 7 years of records reviewed: 2019–2025 $1,000 paid to all 3 nonprofit-corp directors, Dec. 2024

New here? Central Utah GOP House PAC is a political committee that has been moving money — mostly $1,000 checks — to Utah House and Senate Republicans since at least 2019. It shows up in this project because Sen. Brady Brammer (SD-21) is tied to it, but the PAC's own records reveal something more specific: it's registered two different ways with two different state offices, and the people listed in charge aren't the same on both filings.

1. What Is This PAC?

Central Utah GOP House PAC filed its Statement of Organization with the Lieutenant Governor's Office — the filing that actually governs a political committee's conduct under Utah election law — on November 26, 2018. Nearly two years later, on August 25, 2020, an entity by the same name was also registered with the Utah Division of Corporations as a domestic nonprofit corporation. Both filings share a mailing address: 642 Kirby Lane, Suite 105, Spanish Fork.

2. Two Filings, Two Different Rosters

This is the central fact about the PAC's structure: the people who show up as "in charge" depend entirely on which state filing you look at.

Nonprofit-Corp Registration

Utah Division of Corporations · #11914553-0140 · Formed 8/25/2020
  • Registered agent: Brady Brammer, via his own law-practice entity JBB ESQ LLC
  • Director: Brady Brammer
  • Director: Cory Maloy
  • Director: Jon Hawkins

Actual PAC Officers

Statement of Organization · Election Law · Created 11/26/2018
  • Officer: Cory Maloy (Utah House)
  • Officer: Bradley M. Daw (Utah House)
  • Treasurer/CFO: Mike McKell (attorney)
  • Brammer does not appear on this filing.

These are two legally separate documents for two different purposes: the nonprofit-corp filing exists under ordinary corporate law (who can sign a lease, be sued, and so on); the Statement of Organization is the document that actually controls the PAC's campaign-finance conduct under the Election Code. Nothing requires the two rosters to match, and here they mostly don't — Maloy is the only name on both.

The Treasurer tie is the sharper finding. Mike McKell — the PAC's actual Treasurer/CFO — is already documented elsewhere in this project as a $5,000 direct donor to Brammer's 2026 campaign, and as the Senate sponsor of both HB0392 and HB0366, two-thirds of the same 2026 judicial-power stack Brammer chief-sponsored the rest of. Him also personally controlling this PAC's money is a tighter, more concrete relationship than "co-sponsor and donor."

3. Payments to the PAC's Own Nonprofit-Corp Directors

Raw transaction records show that in a single December 2024 reporting batch, all three people listed as directors on the nonprofit-corp filing personally received $1,000 "Donations" payments from the PAC:

Given the PAC's actual controlling officers are Maloy, Daw, and McKell — not Brammer or Hawkins — this reads less like "three directors quietly pay themselves" and more like "the PAC's real officers distributed money to a broad slate of Republicans, which happened to include all three people who also sit on the separate nonprofit-corp registration." Both readings are worth holding at once: it's still true that a PAC one of its own officers helps run paid money to two people (Brammer, Hawkins) who have no official role on the PAC's actual governing document, but do sit on a related corporate shell with the PAC's own address.

6. Worth Asking

This PAC's own directors received money from the PAC they help control — and one of them, Mike McKell, is simultaneously the Senate sponsor of bills this project has separately tracked as concentrating power in the courts. When the people directing a PAC's money and the people benefiting from it are the same small group, is that meaningfully different from a private company's board approving its own bonuses — or is it just how legislative leadership PACs normally work, regardless of party?
